**A CALL — A Pacific AI Talanoa, convened by the Pacific.** Calling all Pacific users of AI — creatives, community organisations, governments, and academics. It is time for a Pacific-owned conversation on artificial intelligence: designed, hosted and governed in the region, through our own institutions, not convened for us by donor countries or by the UN. Geneva gave the world a seat at the table. The Pacific should set its own table. If you would help convene it, get in touch.

For two days in early July, member states of the United Nations sat down together for the first time to talk about how to govern artificial intelligence. The inaugural [Global Dialogue on AI Governance](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en) in Geneva produced no treaty and no binding rules. What it produced instead was direction — and a sentence worth holding onto. Machines, the Secretary-General told delegates, **"can inform, but humans must decide, and answer."**

That is not a throwaway line. It is the whole argument, but the meeting is also worth looking into for who was in the room, who paid for them to be there, and who was missing.

## What Geneva actually decided

The Dialogue was mandated by General Assembly [resolution A/RES/79/325](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en) and co-chaired, by design, by two small states — El Salvador and Estonia. It ran alongside the [Independent International Scientific Panel on AI](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/07/1167862), whose first report warned bluntly that science, in panel co-chair Maria Ressa's framing, faces a hard limit: **"The world cannot govern what it cannot understand."**

The Secretary-General set out four priorities: common safety standards, human-rights red lines, capacity-building for developing countries, and environmental transparency for AI's energy footprint. The closing message from both co-chairs was equally clear: the meeting was a beginning, not an end, and it will be [judged in 2027 not by new declarations but by concrete action](https://dig.watch/updates/global-dialogue-ai-governance-2026-closing) taken in the meantime. On safety, the logic was interoperability — when countries agree how to test systems and assign responsibility, safety travels with the technology; when they do not, a patchwork protects no one.

## Who was in the room — and who wasn't

The scale was real: the UN reported [representation from more than 170 countries](https://dig.watch/updates/un-global-dialogue-ai-governance-opening-session), alongside industry, academia, civil society and the technical community. But a universal forum still reflects the geography of power, and the composition repays a closer look.

**Gender.** At the podium, leadership was relatively balanced. General Assembly President Annalena Baerbock, Dialogue co-chair Egriselda López, Iceland's President Halla Tómasdóttir and scientific-panel co-chair Maria Ressa shared the stage with Guterres, co-chair Rein Tammsaar, Yoshua Bengio and Special Envoy Amandeep Singh Gill. Baerbock used her platform to foreground gendered harm, noting that a [reported 99 per cent of deepfakes are sexual in nature and 96 per cent target women and girls](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/07/1167873). Disaggregated data on overall participation — who actually spoke across the hundreds of delegates — was not published, which is itself a gap in a forum built on inclusion.

**The Pacific.** Harder to find in Geneva itself — but not absent from the region's own record. No prominent Pacific voice featured in the high-level programme, and participation by Small Island Developing States rested on a [discretionary sponsorship fund](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en/participation-support-ai-dialogue-geneva-2026) the organisers described as **"dependent on funds available."** The groundwork at home is real and current: the 2023 [Lagatoi Declaration](https://aiinasia.com/oceania/pacific-islands-digital-growth-governance-foundations-and-regional-cooperation), signed by ICT ministers of thirteen Pacific Island Countries, set regional digital priorities; Papua New Guinea's government has since built an explicit AI agenda around it — [Prime Minister Marape declared at the 2025 Digital Transformation Summit](https://www.pngbusinessnews.com/articles/2026/3/png-government-pushes-digital-transformation-and-ai-agenda-on-safer-internet-day-2026) that ICT and AI "will drive the nation's next 50 years of development," underpinning a Reset@50 governance agenda and a 2025 Social Media Policy that reaches directly into AI use; and the [2nd Pacific ICT Ministers' Dialogue](https://pnghausbung.com/tsiamalili-opens-2nd-pacific-ict-ministers-dialogue/) convened at Pacific Islands Forum headquarters in Suva in August 2025, with ministers naming **digital sovereignty** a shared regional priority. What doesn't yet exist is the AI-specific counterpart — a Pacific gathering that does for AI what the ICT Ministers' Dialogue does for connectivity and infrastructure.

**Indigenous peoples.** Raised as an aspiration more than delivered as structure. Analysts and civil-society submissions pressed for [Global-Majority and Indigenous representation](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/blog/the-case-for-a-global-ai-governance-floor/) inside the working groups "not as outside observers," warning the process could otherwise reproduce the exclusions it names. The Pacific is one of the seven UN Indigenous socio-cultural regions; it was not centred here.

## A data note: even the metrics don't show the Pacific

The Pacific's thin visibility in Geneva has an echo in how AI adoption itself gets measured. Anthropic's [Economic Index](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-geography) tracks Claude usage across 150+ countries against working-age population — an "AI Usage Index" showing wealthy, technologically advanced economies (Israel, Singapore, Australia, New Zealand) using AI several times more than their population share would predict, a pattern independent analysis has shown correlates strongly with GDP per capita. But by the Index's own published methodology, [countries with too few sampled conversations are folded into a catch-all "Minimal" tier](https://assets.anthropic.com/m/218c82b858610fac/original/Economic-Index.pdf) — "we do not know if they have exactly zero usage or little usage that our random sample did not capture." Individual Pacific Island states are not distinguishable in the public country-level data. That is a sampling artefact, not a claim about actual use — but it means the region best positioned to make the case for inclusion is, for now, statistically invisible in the very evidence base that would make the case. That gap is itself a research opportunity for Pacific-led data.

## The political economy underneath

The Dialogue is a genuine multilateral advance, but it sits inside a contest for power. [The United States has resisted multilateral AI rule-making](https://www.csis.org/analysis/what-un-global-dialogue-ai-governance-reveals-about-global-power-shifts); China has backed a UN-centred framework, aligning with the G77; and major technology firms attended as much for legitimacy and market access as for governance. Underneath sits a market concentrated in a handful of companies, and a warning from analysts that without an independent, inclusive evidence base the process risks capture by the best-resourced voices.

For the Pacific, the sharpest fact is structural. The region's presence was contingent on donor funding rather than entitlement — a seat that depends on someone else's cheque. A forum you attend by sponsorship is not yet your forum.

## The line that matters most for the Pacific

That is why the Secretary-General's other warning lands so hard here: the danger of letting the digital divide harden into an AI divide, and the AI divide into a development gap. In the closing plenary, co-chair Egriselda López [recalled a remark from an unnamed "representative of a small country"](https://dig.watch/updates/global-dialogue-ai-governance-2026-closing) that she said captured the spirit of the whole gathering:

"We are here not to be a footnote."

For the Pacific, that is not rhetoric. The region carries heightened exposure to AI's harms — small economies, thin regulatory frameworks, climate vulnerability, and an energy-hungry technology built far away. And there is a structural gap: as it stands, no regional body yet convenes the Pacific specifically on AI.

## A working framework: sovereignty as design authority

Moala Global's position draws directly on an existing lineage. Indigenous Data Sovereignty frameworks — the [CARE Principles](https://ardc.edu.au/resource/the-care-principles/) (Collective Benefit, **Authority to Control**, Responsibility, Ethics) and Australia's Maiam nayri Wingara Communiqué — define data governance as **"the exercise of power and authority over a research project's design, interpretation, processes of validation, ownership/authorship structures."** Moala extends that logic to invoke **sovereignty as design authority**: the communities and institutions that AI leverages, should hold authority over how they are decided about and represented.

University of Melbourne researchers made the same point about AI directly this month, writing Indigenous-led global frameworks establish that [**"communities are the decision-makers when it comes to their own data"**](https://pursuit.unimelb.edu.au/articles/ai-must-be-built-with-indigenous-knowledges,-not-against-them) and that most AI systems in use today were not designed with that in mind. In Aotearoa, [Te Mana Rāraunga](https://www.temanararaunga.maori.nz/principles-of-maori-data-sovereignty), the Māori Data Sovereignty Network, opens its own principles with a whakataukī from Moe Milne of Ngāti Hine — "He taonga te data i tangohia mai i te tangata, i te mea ora" — and advocates for Māori rights and interests in data and its ethical use for the wellbeing of people, language and culture. This is the through-line of everything Moala is building and aims to build for the benefit of the Pacific community.

A Pacific-convened talanoa on AI — bringing together the creatives, community organisations, officials and researchers already using this technology — would do what Geneva structurally cannot: let the Pacific set its own agenda, in its own languages, on its own terms.
