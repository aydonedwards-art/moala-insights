How the world responds to a crisis depends heavily on how that crisis arrives. Fast, visible shocks — a pandemic, a financial collapse — draw a rapid and well-resourced response. Slower crises that unfold in distant or lower-income regions tend to attract far less attention, and far less action, until the consequences are already advanced.

El Niño belongs to the second category, and a strong El Niño is now developing in the Pacific.

The economic cost of the last major El Niño, in the late 1990s, has been estimated at [US$5.7 trillion in lost global income](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adf2983) over the years that followed — comparable in scale to the COVID-19 pandemic. It is rarely remembered that way, because the losses accumulated gradually, fell disproportionately on lower-income countries, and played out over several years rather than weeks. There was no coordinated global response of the kind that scale would ordinarily warrant. The same research projects that, as warming intensifies these events, El Niño-related losses could reach US$84 trillion over this century.

Conditions are again pointing toward a significant event later this year. For Pacific nations, the implications fall on the systems people rely on most directly: water supply, food production and fisheries, health services, and the finances of small island economies. In practice, that means a heightened risk of water shortages, reduced harvests, and disease outbreaks — and consequences that extend beyond economics. Across tropical countries, the risk of new civil conflict has been shown to [roughly double in El Niño years](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10311). These are human security concerns, not only environmental ones.

There is, however, an important difference between El Niño and most other crises: it can be anticipated. The [scientific forecasting](https://www.climate.gov/enso) is well established, and the warning period is both real and usable. The Pacific does not lack forecasts.

What is missing is the step that connects a forecast to a decision — the work of translating 'a strong El Niño is expected' into clear, specific guidance on what communities, governments and partners should do, and when. The evidence for this gap is stark: despite reliable forecasts, [less than 2% of international crisis financing is arranged in advance](https://www.disasterprotection.org/press-releases/leaders-call-for-tenfold-increase-in-proportion-of-international-crisis-finance-that-is-pre-arranged), and only a fraction of a per cent supports acting ahead of a shock at all. As the Centre for Disaster Protection puts it: "less than $1 of every $5,000 of crisis finance goes to low income countries in the form of prearranged finance". The overwhelming majority still flows after the damage is done. This is the gap Moala Global is working to close.

For Australia, and for DFAT's engagement across the Pacific, this represents both a test and an opportunity. The most effective response to El Niño is an early one: financing and plans prepared in advance and aligned to the forecast, rather than assembled after losses occur. The alternative — assistance that arrives once harvests have already failed — is more costly and less effective. The window for early action begins to narrow from around September.

Two principles shape our approach. The first is that early preparation is highly cost-effective: acting ahead of an event costs a fraction of responding to its aftermath. The second is that the response must be Pacific-led. Pacific nations are best placed to direct their own resilience, and the appropriate role for partners is to strengthen existing regional systems and to ensure decision-makers have access to timely, reliable information.

Moala Global was established to work at the intersection of climate, human security and Pacific development, where rigorous and accountable analysis can meaningfully influence outcomes.

The forecasting capability exists, and the period for effective action is open. The central question is whether it is used.
