When the world exploited their oceans for tuna, the Pacific came together and set their own terms. Next is artificial intelligence.

## 1. The proof already exists

In the early 1980s, eight Pacific states formed [the Parties to the Nauru Agreement](https://www.pnatuna.com/content/about-pna) over a single fact: their waters held roughly half the world's skipjack tuna, and distant fleets were taking it for almost nothing. In 2007 they introduced [the Vessel Day Scheme](https://tunapacific.ffa.int/2018/12/07/tuna-diplomacy-is-one-of-the-game-changers-for-the-pacific/) — selling not fish but the right to fish for a day, coordinated across the members under a minimum price no member undercuts. The revenue the islands retained [rose from about US$50 million in 2010 to close to half a billion by 2016](https://foreignpolicy.com/2021/03/05/tuna-fishing-overfishing-conservation-pacific-islands-skipjack-pna/), on a fishery landing some US$2.2 billion a year. Nothing about the ocean changed. The rule about who priced it did.

Hold onto the mechanics, because every part of this paper reuses them: a shared asset, a small number of states, a collective instrument, a floor no member breaks. The question the rest of this paper answers is what happens when you run that play again — not on fish, but on the data and the intelligence economy.

## 2. What the Pacific holds — and the rule about never ceding it

Start with the asset inventory. To see how deep the region's dependence already runs, take one signal the Pacific knows intimately: El Niño. In a companion research matrix compiled for this piece, I mapped the machinery that reads it — every ENSO observing system, index and forecast model — recording for each one who built it, who owns it, where it physically sits, who operates it, and who pays. One pattern runs through every row. The owners, operators and funders are the United States, Japan, Australia and Europe. The Pacific appears only as the place the sensors sit, and as the recipient of the outputs. Not one model is Pacific owned. The single index anchored on Pacific soil — [the sea-level-pressure reading at Tahiti, half of the oldest ENSO index](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/blogs/enso/why-are-there-so-many-enso-indexes-instead-just-one) — is computed offshore.

That dependence matters because the Pacific's ocean holds assets the wider world increasingly wants. Ranked by value to wealthy nations and companies, the region's data assets run: fisheries (already bargained once); [marine genetic resources](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44183-024-00081-7) — the genetic material of ocean life, now a frontier for pharmaceutical and biotech value, with global benefit-sharing rules only [written into the new high-seas biodiversity treaty](https://www.un.org/bbnjagreement/en) this decade; [seabed-mineral surveys](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12974) — the Pacific seabed holds the critical minerals the energy transition and the AI hardware build-out both depend on, and mining companies and governments are already circling it; strategic geography; and the region's own indigenous knowledge systems. The raw climate observations sit last — not because they lack value, but because international rules already oblige their free exchange, and because a warning that protects the world should stay free.

[In an AI economy, value is not captured at the data layer — it accrues in the layers above it](https://www.amadeuscapital.com/ai-commoditisation-curve/): in the compute that hosts the data, in the products built from it, and in the models trained on it, where no open licence reaches. A region can give away its data 'openly' and watch the return accrue entirely to whoever owns the layers above. So the bloc's standing rule is: contribute the climate commons freely, and never cede the sovereign classes — the fisheries records, the genetics, the seabed, the geography, the knowledge systems. Not because openness is wrong, but because openness at the bottom with ownership at the top is dispossession wearing the clothes of a public good.

## 3. Who owns the mechanism — and why that is a choice, not a fate

Holding the asset is not the same as holding the machinery that reads it, and the Pacific currently holds neither the machinery nor the terms. For a picture of the alternative, look at how Australia itself behaves when a foreign machine sits on its soil. Australia hosts Pine Gap, a United States intelligence facility [whose higher management has always been American](https://nautilus.org/napsnet/napsnet-special-reports/the-higher-management-of-pine-gap/). Canberra pursued a doctrine it named ['full knowledge and concurrence', formalised in 1976](https://www.aspistrategist.org.au/pine-gap-50-paradox-joint-facility/), defended it for forty years, and placed Australian officials inside the operation to give the words force. It treated hosting a foreign machine as a sovereignty negotiation, not a favour.

The Pacific hosts the world's climate observatory in its own exclusive economic zones (EEZ), and its legal position is seemingly stronger than Australia's ever was. Under [Part XIII of the law of the sea](https://www.un.org/depts/los/convention_agreements/texts/unclos/part13.htm), marine research in those zones requires the coastal state's consent and gives it a right to participate and to the resulting data. In the EEZ that consent is qualified — states are to grant it in normal circumstances — but it may be refused for research 'of direct significance for the exploration and exploitation of natural resources', which is precisely the category the sovereign data classes fall into. The rights are there, unused. The deficit is not access or capacity. It is that no one has yet made hosting a sovereignty question.

And the word 'capacity' is doing quiet work in keeping it that way. [Fabinyi, Evans and Foale](https://www.ecologyandsociety.org/vol19/iss4/art28/) showed that technocratic 'resilience' and 'capacity' framings of environmental governance hide the real question — power: whose knowledge counts, whose voice is privileged or silenced. Writing on the Pacific aid system itself, [Taylor, Middleby and Vunibola reach the same verdict — that its recent disruptions amount to "not transformation but consolidation" of an extractive core](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/dpr.70030). Calling the Pacific's position a capacity gap is not a neutral description. It is the frame that keeps ownership offshore.

## 4. What others have already built

Change the frame from capacity to bargaining, and the Pacific does not start from zero. It joins a decade of practice — and it can borrow the parts that work. (Each model links to its source.)

| Model | What it is | What it achieved | What the Pacific borrows |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Māori — Te Mana Raraunga](https://www.temanararaunga.maori.nz/) | Māori Data Sovereignty Network (2016), grounded in Te Tiriti + UNDRIP; data as taonga | Mana Ōrite Agreement with Stats NZ (a first); Te Kāhui Raraunga to run it; Māori Data Governance Model (2023) | The pathway: network → principles → binding agreement with the state → operational body |
| [Aboriginal & TSI — Maiam nayri Wingara](https://www.maiamnayriwingara.org/) | Data Sovereignty Collective (2017); principles set at the 2018 Canberra Summit; UNDRIP-based | Embedded in the National Agreement on Closing the Gap (Priority Reform Four) and an APS-wide Indigenous Data Governance framework | How to move principles into binding national policy; the control-the-ecosystem framing |
| [African Union — Data Policy Framework](https://au.int/en/documents/20220728/au-data-policy-framework) | Continental framework (2022) for a bloc of developing states; tied to the AfCFTA and AU AI Strategy | A united front to become 'standard makers, not standard takers'; collective bargaining against big tech | The developing-state bloc model — harmonise nationally, negotiate as one |
| [European Union — GDPR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels_effect) | A single regulatory regime across a bloc of states | The 'Brussels effect': a bloc large enough to set global rules others must follow | Regulation as leverage: shared rules become a bargaining asset |
| [Gaia-X](https://gaia-x.eu/) | European federated, sovereign data-infrastructure initiative | An attempt to build shared infrastructure rather than rent it from foreign clouds | The infrastructure-sovereignty ambition — and its difficulty |
| [DEPA](https://www.mfat.govt.nz/en/trade/free-trade-agreements/free-trade-agreements-in-force/digital-economy-partnership-agreement-depa/overview) | Digital Economy Partnership Agreement — founded by NZ, Singapore and Chile (2020); now expanding (South Korea acceded) | Proof that small, non-aligned states can write plurilateral digital rules together | A ready template a Pacific bloc could join or mirror — NZ is already in it |

## 5. The Pacific's unique position

The two closest precedents in that table are the indigenous ones. Māori, through Te Mana Raraunga, and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, through Maiam nayri Wingara, each built the same pathway: a network, a set of principles, a binding relationship with the state, an operational body. Both moved their peoples from being counted to doing the counting.

But both must act as peoples inside a state, negotiating with a Crown or a Commonwealth for recognition of authority over data about them. Pacific nations are not inside anyone's state. They are states — with territory, treaties, exclusive economic zones and a vote. That is the multiplier. It lets the Pacific take the indigenous blueprint ([CARE-based principles](https://datascience.codata.org/articles/10.5334/dsj-2020-043), community authority, data as a living inheritance) and fuse it with the collective-bargaining structure the Nauru Agreement already proved, backed by the one thing indigenous data movements elsewhere lack: full international sovereignty. Indigenous values, tuna-bloc structure, state power. No other indigenous data movement has all three to draw on.

## 6. The bloc — who, what, how, when

**Who convenes it.** The Pacific Islands Forum at the apex — its [2050 Strategy for the Blue Pacific Continent](https://forumsec.org/2050) already names Pacific states 'custodians' of their ocean and carries a Technology and Connectivity pathway to hang this on. The Parties to the Nauru Agreement as the nucleus: eight states that already bargain collectively and trust the model. The Pacific Community, the Forum Fisheries Agency, SPREP and the University of the South Pacific as the machinery — proof the region can run shared technical institutions.
